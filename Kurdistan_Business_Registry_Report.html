EXHIBIT 01 / 07 — ACTIVITY TYPES

Every company in Kurdistan's registry declares one or more official activities — the specific line(s) of business it's licensed for. Out of nearly 1,939 distinct activity types on the books, from mechanical contracting to falcon breeding, one answer dwarfs everything else.

EXHIBIT 02 / 07 — THE CASE FILE

This isn't a random preference. Six structural features of the Kurdistan Region's economy point the same direction, and together they explain why a shop that resells goods is a far easier bet than a factory that makes them.

01 — THE FLOOR IS $760

Registering costs almost nothing

The legal minimum capital to register a company in the KRI is exactly 1,000,000 IQD (~$760). 58% of every company in this dataset registers at precisely that floor. No factory, machinery or import license required — just a shopfront and a supplier.

02 — AN IMPORT ECONOMY

Imports run the shelves

Imports make up roughly 85% of the Region's estimated $5–5.5B in annual external trade, and most of what comes in is consumed locally rather than re-exported as value-added product — a market built to move goods, not make them.

03 — OIL FUNDS THE CART

Petroleum pays for the shopping

Government revenue is overwhelmingly tied to petroleum, funding a consumption economy on the back of resource rents rather than broad-based manufacturing — the textbook pattern of a trade-heavy, production-light private sector.

04 — A 6,000-YEAR CROSSROADS

Geography did the marketing

Trade routes have run through this land for millennia. The Region borders Turkey and Iran directly — its two largest trading partners — and sits as a natural gateway into the rest of Iraq.

05 — FACTORIES CAN'T COMPETE

Industry lacks the basics

Unreliable electricity and water infrastructure deters manufacturers and leaves industry uncompetitive against imports; small family workshops still make up most of what industrial production exists at all.

06 — ONE LICENSE, ANY GOOD

Trading is built to pivot

A "General Trading" license is a blank check — it lets an owner sell whatever is profitable this season, without re-registering. In a market that moves with oil prices and currency swings, that flexibility is worth a lot.

Erbil District alone — just one of dozens of districts in the registry — accounts for 54.7% of every company filed. Within it, the small Christian quarter of Ankawa, better known for its hotels, churches and nightlife, is home to 156 registered companies in its own right. Trading intensity peaks not in the capital but out in Zakho, on the Turkish border, where 54.7% of all companies are General Traders — likely a reflection of its role as a land-border crossing point.

CONTEXT

A wider labor-force gap

Only around 15% of working-age women in Iraq are in the labor force at all — one of the lowest rates worldwide — and up to 75% of employed Kurdish women work in the public sector. Female-run private companies are rare well beyond this dataset.

OWNERSHIP

Mostly solo operations

Among companies with shareholder records, 70.3% are wholly owned by a single person. Kurdish business here is overwhelmingly personal and family-run rather than broadly syndicated.

NAMES ON FILE

13,671 names, 15,541 companies

Most CEOs appear once. But 1,250 names recur across two or more filings — some likely serial founders, though Kurdish naming conventions (given name + father's + grandfather's name, no fixed surname) mean shared names don't always mean the same person.

FOREIGN MONEY

It mostly arrives as a shareholder, not a flag

Only 2.0% of companies are formally registered as "Foreign Company" origin — but foreign nationals show up as a shareholder in 13.3% of all shareholder listings. Foreign capital tends to sit inside a nominally local company, not a foreign-flagged one.

THE FLOOR, AGAIN

Median capital: 1,000,000 IQD

Half of every company in the registry — the flashy multinationals included — is at or below the exact legal minimum. The 90th percentile is still only $382,000; it takes the top 1% to clear roughly $3 million.

About this report

Built from a filing-level extract of the Kurdistan Region business registry: 15,541 companies across Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, Duhok, Zakho, Raparin, Garmian, Soran and Halabja, dated between 1998 and August 6, 2026. Fields include CEO name and gender, activity types, registered capital, company structure, and shareholder records. This is an independent analysis of that extract, not an official government publication.
